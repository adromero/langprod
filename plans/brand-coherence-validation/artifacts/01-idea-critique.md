# Devil's Advocate: Idea Review

## Core Understanding

The proposal is to repurpose a failed hypothesis test into a commercial methodology: use LLM hidden-state geometry (specifically RSA on extracted embeddings from Qwen2.5-32B's late layers) to quantify how consistently a brand's messaging survives translation across communication channels, sell this as a $25-75K consulting engagement ("coherence audit"), and validate the approach through five sequential experiments testing real-document sensitivity, channel attribution, attribute drill-down, temporal drift detection, and competitive benchmarking. The pivot is grounded in the finding that while register dominates representational geometry, product identity signal does exist at late layers (RSA r=0.371 at layer 61), and this signal could serve as the basis for measuring cross-channel coherence.

## Challenged Assumptions

1. **"Product identity clustering = messaging coherence."** The experiment showed that the model clusters products by identity at late layers (RSA r=0.371). But this is product *identity* --- the model recognizing "this is about Colgate Total" --- not messaging *coherence*. A product with wildly inconsistent messaging across channels could still cluster tightly if the model recognizes it by name, unique ingredients, or category markers. Coherence (as brand managers understand it --- consistent tone, emphasis, claims hierarchy, emotional positioning) is a much more nuanced construct than "the model can tell these documents are about the same product." The metric may measure product recognizability rather than message consistency.

2. **"The calibration dataset provides ground truth for coherence measurement."** The 800 stimuli were LLM-generated, controlled to 80-150 words, and designed to convey *all core attributes* in every register variant. This is the *opposite* of what real cross-channel messaging looks like. A tweet omits ingredients. A regulatory filing omits emotional positioning. A consumer review focuses on one experience. The calibration dataset tells you what "same product, same information, different surface form" looks like --- it does not tell you what "same product, different information, different surface form" looks like, which is exactly what a real coherence audit needs to detect.

3. **"Register dominance is a nuisance to control for, not a fatal problem."** The experiment found register RSA r=0.670 vs. product RSA r=0.371. Register is nearly 2x stronger as an organizing principle. In real documents, the register signal will be even stronger because real-world channels differ more dramatically than the controlled stimuli (a 280-character tweet vs. a 3000-word regulatory filing). The plan does not describe any method for factoring out register before measuring product coherence. Without explicit register correction, the "coherence metric" will largely measure "how similar are these channels in general" rather than "how consistently does this specific brand communicate across channels." Every brand's social media will look far from their regulatory filings, coherent or not.

4. **"A sample of 5 vs. 5 products provides sufficient statistical power."** Experiment 1 proposes comparing 5 "known consistent" and 5 "known inconsistent" products with a pass criterion of "no overlap, or at worst one misclassification." With n=5 per group, the probability of achieving clean separation by chance (assuming the metric is random noise) is non-trivial. More importantly, any single confound (e.g., one "consistent" brand happens to be in a highly regulated category with narrow vocabulary, producing tight clustering for the wrong reason) could flip the result. The design has no statistical power analysis and no way to distinguish "the metric works" from "we got lucky with product selection."

5. **"Professional judgment reliably identifies 'known consistent' and 'known inconsistent' products."** The ground truth for Experiment 1 is hand-picked: you select products you believe have consistent or inconsistent messaging. This introduces confirmation bias in product selection. If the metric agrees with your prior judgment, it could mean the metric works, or it could mean you (unconsciously) selected products that differ on some confounding dimension (brand size, category, marketing budget) that the metric picks up instead of coherence. There is no blinding in the experimental design.

6. **"Layer 61 representations are suitable for real-document analysis."** The layer 61 product-identity peak was found on 80-150 word LLM-generated stimuli. Real documents range from ~20 words (tweets) to thousands of words (regulatory filings). Mean pooling across token positions will produce very different representation geometries for documents of vastly different lengths. The optimal layer for real documents may be completely different, or the signal may vanish entirely. The plan acknowledges length variation as a concern but treats it as a "failure mode to watch for" rather than as a design parameter to systematically test.

7. **"Cosine distance on mean-pooled hidden states is the right metric."** Mean pooling collapses sequence-level structure into a single vector, losing all information about what is said where in the document. Two documents could discuss the same attributes but in completely different order, emphasis, and framing --- and produce identical mean-pooled representations. Conversely, a document that mentions a product's key attribute once in passing (among many other topics) will have that signal diluted by the other content. Mean pooling may be too lossy for the granularity the methodology claims to offer.

8. **"The approach is model-agnostic enough for a commercial offering."** The entire methodology is calibrated on Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct-GPTQ-Int4. Different models will have different layer dynamics, different anisotropy profiles, and potentially different register/content trade-offs. If you move to a newer or better model (inevitable over the timeline of a consulting business), you may need to recalibrate everything. The plan does not address model portability or what happens when Qwen2.5-32B becomes obsolete.

9. **"Consumer reviews are a valid 'channel' on equal footing with brand-controlled channels."** Consumer reviews are not authored by the brand. Measuring whether a consumer review is "coherent" with marketing copy is measuring something fundamentally different from whether the brand's own messaging is coherent across channels it controls. A perfect brand could still have divergent consumer reviews. Including consumer reviews as a channel conflates brand messaging discipline with market reception.

10. **"The sequential experiment design is efficient."** The plan says "we don't move to the next until the current one passes." But Experiment 2 (channel attribution) is just a reanalysis of Experiment 1 data --- it should be done simultaneously. Experiment 3 (attribute drill-down) requires fundamentally different probe design and may reveal that the entire measurement approach needs changing. If Experiment 3 fails, the channel attribution from Experiment 2 might still be valuable. The strict sequential gating assumes independence between experiments that does not exist.

## Gaps Identified

1. **No register correction methodology.** The single most important technical gap. The original experiment found register dominates product identity at every layer. The plan proposes measuring cross-channel coherence *without specifying how to separate register signal from coherence signal.* This is like trying to measure someone's height while they're standing on platforms of different heights and you haven't figured out how to measure the platforms. The plan needs an explicit register normalization approach --- possibly partial RSA controlling for register (which the codebase already supports via `partial_rsa`), or representation alignment techniques, or within-register baselines. Without this, Experiment 1 will almost certainly fail because all products will show low cross-channel similarity (dominated by register) or the metric will just measure "how much jargon does this brand use in regulatory filings."

2. **No definition of the coherence metric itself.** The plan describes the general idea (extract representations, measure clustering) but never specifies: Which layer(s)? What pooling? What distance metric? How do you aggregate pairwise distances into a single "coherence score"? Is it the mean cosine similarity across all channel pairs? The determinant of the similarity matrix? The minimum pairwise similarity? These choices will dramatically affect results, and they need to be specified *before* running Experiment 1, not tuned post-hoc (which would be p-hacking).

3. **No baseline comparator for Experiment 1.** If the coherence metric separates the two groups, how do you know it's better than simpler alternatives? You should run parallel baselines: TF-IDF cosine similarity across channels, BERTScore, or even simple keyword overlap. If a bag-of-words approach achieves the same separation, the LLM hidden-state methodology adds no value. This is especially important given that the original experiment found 100% BoW accuracy on all classification tasks --- surface features may be sufficient.

4. **No specification for handling document length variation.** Real documents span orders of magnitude in length. A regulatory filing for a sunscreen might be 5000 words. A tweet might be 30 words. Mean pooling a 5000-word document into a 5120-dimensional vector vs. a 30-word document will produce representations dominated by length effects. The plan needs a length normalization strategy: truncation, chunking, sliding window, or extraction of matched-length segments.

5. **No inter-rater reliability check for the "ground truth."** Experiments 1, 2, 3, and 5 all depend on human judgment as ground truth (which products are consistent/inconsistent, which channel is the outlier, which attributes are missing, which ranking is correct). But there is no plan to measure whether multiple humans agree on these judgments. If inter-rater reliability is low, the ground truth is noise, and the experiments cannot validate anything.

6. **No power analysis or effect size estimation.** With 10 products in Experiment 1, 5 in Experiment 2, 3 in Experiment 3, 2-3 in Experiment 4, and 5 in Experiment 5 --- these are all extremely small samples. The plan does not estimate what effect size would be needed to detect with these sample sizes, nor does it acknowledge that negative results at these sample sizes are inconclusive (failure to detect an effect is not evidence the effect doesn't exist).

7. **No plan for what "coherence score" means numerically.** Is it an absolute value (0-1)? A percentile relative to the calibration dataset? A z-score? How do you communicate to a client what "your coherence score is 0.73" means? The calibration dataset could theoretically provide a reference distribution, but the plan doesn't specify how to bridge from raw RSA values to interpretable scores.

8. **No consideration of document preprocessing.** Real documents contain boilerplate (copyright notices, navigation elements scraped from web pages, Amazon listing templates, social media hashtags and mentions). These structural elements will dominate the representation and have nothing to do with messaging coherence. The plan needs a document cleaning specification.

9. **Missing: What happens to the metric when you add a new channel?** If a client wants to include TikTok video transcripts, podcast mentions, or in-store signage photos (OCR'd), does the metric remain stable? The plan tests a fixed set of channels but doesn't address extensibility.

10. **No competitive or prior art analysis.** Brand consistency measurement is not a new idea. Firms like Brandwatch, Sprinklr, and BrandGuard already offer quantitative brand consistency metrics (typically based on sentiment analysis, topic modeling, and NLP classifiers). The plan does not compare the proposed approach against existing tools or articulate what the hidden-state method captures that these tools miss. A client spending $25-75K will ask "why this instead of Brandwatch?"

## Risk Scenarios

1. **Register signal drowns coherence signal in real documents (high likelihood, high impact).** Real-world channel documents differ far more in surface form than the controlled 80-150 word stimuli. A regulatory filing and a TikTok caption are so stylistically different that the register dimension will completely dominate the representational geometry. Every brand will look "incoherent" because channels are inherently dissimilar, making the metric useless for comparative purposes. This is the most likely failure mode and the plan's greatest vulnerability.

2. **The metric measures vocabulary overlap, not semantic coherence (high likelihood, medium impact).** The original experiment found 100% BoW accuracy. If mean-pooled hidden states at late layers are largely encoding lexical content (which is plausible --- late layers are preparing for next-token prediction), then the "coherence metric" reduces to "how much vocabulary do these documents share." A brand that uses consistent jargon across channels would score high regardless of whether the underlying message is coherent. A brand that deliberately adapts its vocabulary per channel (good marketing practice) would score low.

3. **Experiment 1 passes for the wrong reason (medium likelihood, high impact).** The 5 "known consistent" brands might be large, established brands with extensive documentation (more text, more channels, more corporate boilerplate). The 5 "known inconsistent" brands might be small startups with sparse documentation. The metric might separate them based on documentation completeness or brand maturity rather than messaging coherence. With n=5, you cannot control for these confounds.

4. **Model obsolescence breaks the calibration (medium likelihood, medium impact).** The methodology depends on Qwen2.5-32B's specific layer dynamics. When this model is superseded (months, not years), the entire calibration dataset and layer selection become invalid. Rebuilding on a new model is feasible (the pipeline supports it) but means the "validated methodology" requires periodic revalidation --- a significant operational cost for a consulting offering.

5. **The concept of "messaging coherence" is not well-defined enough for quantification (medium likelihood, high impact).** The plan acknowledges this in Experiment 5's failure mode, but it deserves more emphasis. Brand managers may use "messaging coherence" to mean very different things: consistent visual identity (not capturable by text analysis), consistent emotional tone, consistent claims hierarchy, consistent target audience framing, or simply using the same product name. The metric will capture some weighted combination of these, and different clients will weight them differently. A single number may not be actionable.

6. **Experiment 3 (attribute drill-down) is methodologically unsound (high likelihood, medium impact).** Creating "probe stimuli" that isolate individual attributes is essentially asking: "does this LLM-generated sentence about SPF 50 have high cosine similarity to this paragraph from a regulatory filing that mentions SPF 50?" This is likely dominated by keyword overlap at the embedding level, not deep semantic similarity. If the probe contains "SPF 50" and the document contains "SPF 50," they'll be similar. If the probe says "sun protection factor" and the document says "SPF 50," they might not be. This test may validate keyword search, not semantic coherence measurement.

7. **Temporal analysis confounded by web language evolution (medium likelihood, low-medium impact).** Experiment 4 measures coherence across time points. But web writing style has changed substantially (shorter sentences, more emoji, different SEO practices). A brand whose messaging hasn't changed at all will still show different coherence scores at different time points because the *baseline* register characteristics of web writing have shifted. The plan mentions this but proposes only a single "control product" as a mitigation --- insufficient for separating brand-specific drift from secular trends.

8. **Expert validation in Experiment 5 is unfalsifiable as designed (medium likelihood, medium impact).** The pass criterion is "mostly correct (at least 4 of 5 brands ranked plausibly)." With only 5 brands and vague criteria ("plausible"), experts are likely to find some rationalization for any ranking. People are good at post-hoc explanation. A stronger test would ask experts to *predict* the ranking before seeing it, or would use a forced-choice design where experts evaluate specific pairwise comparisons.

## Key Questions for the User

1. **How will you separate register signal from coherence signal?** The original experiment showed register dominates at every layer. What specific technique will you use to ensure the coherence metric isn't just measuring "how different are regulatory filings from tweets in general"? Will you use partial RSA (already in the codebase) to control for register? Will you compute within-register baselines? This is the single most important technical decision and it's unspecified. --- *Priority: high*

2. **What is the exact definition of the coherence metric?** Before running any experiment, you need to commit to a formula: which layers, what pooling, what distance, how aggregated into a single score. If you tune these after seeing Experiment 1 results, you're fitting to noise. Can you pre-register the metric definition? --- *Priority: high*

3. **Have you tested what your existing pipeline produces when you feed it documents of radically different lengths?** Take one of your 80-product calibration stimuli, truncate it to 20 words, and expand it to 500 words. Does the similarity to the original change in a predictable way? This is a 30-minute test that could save days. --- *Priority: high*

4. **Why not run a simple baseline alongside Experiment 1?** TF-IDF cosine similarity, sentence-BERT embeddings, or even BM25 keyword overlap across channels. If these achieve comparable separation to the hidden-state method, the entire LLM extraction pipeline adds complexity without value. --- *Priority: high*

5. **How do you define "known consistent" and "known inconsistent" operationally?** What specific, observable criteria distinguish the two groups? Can you write them down before selecting products? Would a second person, using the same criteria, select the same products? --- *Priority: high*

6. **Have you considered using representation differences *within* a layer rather than raw distances?** For example: compute the centroid of all Channel X documents and all Channel Y documents for a product, then measure the angle between the product-specific displacement vectors. This would factor out channel-level effects and isolate product-specific channel divergence. --- *Priority: medium*

7. **What is the minimum effect size you need for a commercially useful metric?** If two brands differ by 0.02 in coherence score, is that actionable? What about 0.1? Knowing the minimum useful effect size determines whether your sample sizes are adequate. --- *Priority: medium*

8. **Why Qwen2.5-32B specifically, and what's the model migration plan?** If you build a commercial offering on this model's representations, you're locked to it. Have you verified that the product-identity signal exists at comparable strength in other models? --- *Priority: medium*

9. **Should consumer reviews be in the coherence metric at all?** They are not brand-authored. Including them measures market reception, not brand discipline. This changes the product from "how well does your team maintain message consistency" to "how well does your message survive contact with consumers" --- a different value proposition. --- *Priority: medium*

10. **Is there a faster path to testing the core assumption?** Before building a 5-experiment validation sequence, could you take 2 products (one "consistent," one "inconsistent"), collect their real documents, run them through the existing pipeline in an afternoon, and see if anything sensible comes out? A 2-hour proof-of-concept could prevent 2 weeks of wasted effort. --- *Priority: medium*

11. **How will you handle documents that mention multiple products?** A brand's landing page might describe a product line. A consumer review might compare two competing products. A social media post might mention a product alongside an unrelated topic. The plan assumes one document = one product, which is often false. --- *Priority: low*

12. **What is the plan for anisotropy correction on real documents?** The original writeup (Section 5.3) explicitly flags anisotropy as a limitation. The codebase supports mean centering and whitening. Has the analysis been rerun with anisotropy correction? The product-identity signal might look very different after correction. --- *Priority: low*

## Alternative Perspectives

**Use the model as a feature extractor, not the whole methodology.** Instead of RSA on hidden states (which inherits all the register-dominance problems), extract late-layer representations and train a supervised model to predict coherence scores from labeled examples. This requires ground-truth labels, but even 50-100 labeled brand-channel pairs could train a simple regression model. The hidden states become features, not the metric itself. This sidesteps the register-dominance problem because the supervised model can learn to ignore register-correlated dimensions.

**Flip the register dominance from bug to feature.** If the model organizes by register above all else, you could measure how far a specific brand's documents deviate from the "typical" register prototype. A brand whose marketing copy looks like typical marketing copy has register-appropriate messaging. A brand whose marketing copy drifts toward regulatory language has a coherence problem. The metric becomes "register appropriateness per channel" rather than "cross-channel similarity." This would actually leverage the model's strongest signal rather than fighting against it.

**Use contrastive fine-tuning to create a coherence-specific embedding space.** Take the calibration dataset (800 stimuli with known product-register structure) and fine-tune a smaller model with a contrastive loss that pulls same-product representations together and pushes different-product representations apart, *regardless of register.* This would create an embedding space where register is explicitly factored out and product coherence is the dominant axis. This is more engineering work but addresses the register-dominance problem at its root.

## Overall Assessment

The plan is well-structured and shows genuine intellectual honesty --- the sequential gating, the explicit failure modes, the decision framework, and the willingness to kill the project if Experiment 1 fails are all commendable. The pivot from a rejected hypothesis to a commercial application is creative and potentially valuable.

However, the single biggest risk is that the plan proposes to measure cross-channel coherence using a representation space where *channel identity is the dominant signal.* The original experiment proved, convincingly, that register dwarfs product identity in the model's geometry. The plan then proposes to use that same geometry to measure something that requires *seeing through* register differences to the product signal underneath --- without specifying any technique for factoring out the register signal. This is the plan's central contradiction, and until it is resolved (via partial RSA, register normalization, contrastive fine-tuning, or some other explicit method), the entire experiment sequence is likely to produce results dominated by channel effects rather than coherence effects.

The single most important question to answer before proceeding is: **What is your specific, pre-registered method for separating register/channel signal from product coherence signal?** If you cannot answer this question concretely, Experiment 1 will either fail outright (all products look incoherent because channels are inherently dissimilar) or succeed for the wrong reasons (it measures vocabulary overlap, not semantic coherence).
