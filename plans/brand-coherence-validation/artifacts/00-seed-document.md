# Brand Message Coherence: Research Plan

## The Idea

A quantitative research methodology that measures how consistently a product's core message survives translation across communication channels — from R&D specification through regulatory filing, marketing copy, retail shelf description, and consumer conversation.

Today this is assessed qualitatively: someone reads all the materials and makes a judgment call. We replace the judgment call with a number, derived from how a large language model internally represents the same product across different communication styles.

The deliverable is a consulting engagement, not a software product. A brand team hires us to run a coherence audit on their product portfolio. We ingest their materials across channels, run the analysis, and produce a report showing where their messaging is aligned, where it's drifting, and what's getting lost in translation. Price point: $25-75K per brand study, consistent with existing market research pricing.

## Background

### What we discovered

We ran an experiment testing the "Protocol Layer Hypothesis" — the idea that transformer middle layers encode a format-agnostic semantic substrate where the same product, described in different registers (marketing, regulatory, casual, patent, journalistic), converges in representational geometry.

The hypothesis was rejected. But the experiment produced a working measurement instrument:

- **800 stimuli** generated: 80 products (40 real CPG, 40 fictional) x 5 registers x 2 paraphrase variants
- **Hidden state extraction** from Qwen2.5-32B across all 64 layers (800 x 65 x 5120 representations)
- **Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA)** quantifying how product identity and register identity organize the model's internal geometry
- **Linear probes** classifying product, category, and register from layer representations

Key findings relevant to the coherence methodology:

1. **Register is the dominant organizing principle** at every layer (RSA r=0.670 for register vs r=0.371 for product identity). The model's internal map of product descriptions is organized primarily by *how* things are said, not *what* they're about.

2. **Product identity signal exists but peaks late** (layer 61 of 64, RSA r=0.371). Fine-grained "which product is this" information is geometrically present, concentrated in the final layers.

3. **Category identity saturates early** (probe F1=0.99 by layer 20). The model quickly determines "this is about oral care" and maintains that signal throughout.

4. **Register is trivially classifiable everywhere** (probe F1=1.000 at all layers, including the embedding layer). Surface-form register cues are so strong they're never stripped away.

5. **Within-category product discrimination peaks late** (RSA r=0.198 at layer 61). The model can distinguish Colgate Total from Crest Pro-Health, but only in the final processing stages.

### What this means for coherence measurement

The experiment gives us two things:

**A measurement technique.** Take multiple descriptions of the same product (from different channels/registers), extract their LLM representations, and measure how tightly they cluster. High clustering = consistent messaging. Low clustering = the message is drifting across channels.

**A calibration dataset.** 800 stimuli with fully crossed product identity and register, providing ground truth for what "same product, different register" looks like in representational space vs. "different product, same register."

### What we don't yet know

The experiment tested LLM-generated descriptions of products with controlled attributes. A real coherence audit would use *actual client documents* — real marketing copy, real regulatory filings, real consumer reviews. These differ from our stimuli in critical ways:

- They weren't generated by an LLM (different distributional properties)
- They vary in length far more than our controlled 80-150 word stimuli
- They may describe products at different levels of completeness (a tweet doesn't mention every ingredient)
- The "ground truth" alignment between documents isn't known — we'd need to establish that a given marketing claim and a given regulatory passage actually describe the same product feature

These unknowns define the validation experiments below.

## What We Need to Test

Five experiments, in priority order. Each has a clear pass/fail criterion. We don't move to the next until the current one passes. We don't think about building a platform or selling engagements until all five pass.

### Experiment 1: Real-Document Sensitivity

**Question:** Does the coherence metric actually distinguish products with known-consistent messaging from products with known-inconsistent messaging?

**Method:**
1. Select 5 CPG products where we know (from professional judgment or public information) that the messaging is tightly controlled across channels — e.g., a major pharmaceutical OTC brand with strict regulatory alignment
2. Select 5 CPG products where we know messaging has drifted — e.g., a startup brand whose social media voice is disconnected from their packaging claims
3. For each product, collect 3-5 real documents from different channels: the product's regulatory filing, marketing landing page, Amazon listing, a consumer review, and a social media post
4. Run the coherence analysis on all 10 products
5. Compare the coherence scores for the "known consistent" set vs. the "known inconsistent" set

**Pass criterion:** The coherence metric separates the two groups with no overlap, or at worst one misclassification out of 10. If the metric can't distinguish products that a human expert can easily separate, it's not measuring anything useful.

**Failure mode to watch for:** If all products score similarly (either all high or all low), the metric has no discriminative power. This likely means length variation, completeness differences, or domain vocabulary are swamping the product identity signal.

**Estimated effort:** 2-3 days (mostly document collection)

### Experiment 2: Channel Attribution

**Question:** When coherence is low, can we identify *which* channel is the outlier?

**Method:**
1. Take the 5 "known inconsistent" products from Experiment 1
2. For each, compute pairwise coherence between every pair of channel documents (regulatory-marketing, regulatory-social, marketing-social, etc.)
3. For at least 3 of the 5 products, we should have a professional judgment about which channel is the outlier (e.g., "the social media voice is disconnected from everything else")
4. Check whether the pairwise coherence matrix correctly identifies the same outlier channel

**Pass criterion:** The pairwise analysis correctly identifies the outlier channel for at least 3 of 5 products. If it identifies a *different* channel as the outlier, investigate whether it found a real misalignment that the human judgment missed (possible) or whether the metric is noise (likely).

**Failure mode to watch for:** If the pairwise matrix is uniform (all pairs roughly equally coherent or incoherent), the metric can't do channel-level attribution, which makes it far less useful as a diagnostic tool.

**Estimated effort:** 1-2 days (analysis of Experiment 1 data, no new collection needed)

### Experiment 3: Attribute-Level Drill-Down

**Question:** Can we identify *what content* is getting lost in translation, not just that something is?

**Method:**
1. Select 3 products with known attribute-level messaging gaps (e.g., a sunscreen whose SPF number is prominent in regulatory filings and packaging but absent from social media; a food product whose protein content is featured in marketing but not on the retail listing)
2. Generate probe stimuli: for each product, create minimal descriptions that isolate individual attributes (one description per key attribute)
3. Compute the representational similarity between each attribute probe and each real channel document
4. Check whether the attributes known to be missing from specific channels show lower similarity scores for those channels

**Pass criterion:** For at least 2 of 3 products, the attribute-level similarity correctly identifies which attributes are present vs. absent in which channels, matching human judgment.

**Failure mode to watch for:** If the attribute probes are too short or too decontextualized to produce meaningful representations, this approach won't work. We may need to test different probe formulations (single sentence vs. short paragraph, with vs. without product context).

**Estimated effort:** 3-4 days (requires careful probe design and iterative testing)

### Experiment 4: Temporal Coherence Drift

**Question:** Can the metric detect messaging drift over time — e.g., a brand whose messaging was coherent at launch but has drifted as different teams created materials independently?

**Method:**
1. Select 2-3 products with accessible historical marketing materials (Wayback Machine captures, press release archives, old Amazon listings)
2. Collect materials from at least 3 time points (launch, 1 year post-launch, current)
3. Compute coherence at each time point
4. Check whether coherence changes over time match known brand management events (agency change, brand refresh, category expansion)

**Pass criterion:** Coherence trajectories are non-random and correspond to known brand management events for at least 2 of 3 products. This doesn't require exact correspondence — just that the metric moves in an interpretable direction when we know something changed.

**Failure mode to watch for:** If the metric is dominated by general language change over time (web writing style evolves) rather than brand-specific messaging drift, it won't be useful as a diagnostic tool. We'd need to control for this by comparing against a "control" product in the same category that didn't change its messaging.

**Estimated effort:** 3-5 days (historical document collection is time-consuming)

### Experiment 5: Competitive Coherence Benchmarking

**Question:** Does the metric produce useful competitive comparisons — e.g., "Brand A's messaging is more coherent than Brand B's in the same category"?

**Method:**
1. Select one CPG category (e.g., toothpaste or protein bars) with 5+ competing brands
2. For each brand, collect the same set of channel documents (regulatory, marketing, retail listing, social media, consumer review)
3. Compute coherence scores for all brands
4. Present the competitive coherence ranking to 2-3 industry professionals (brand managers or category analysts) and ask: "Does this ranking match your intuition about which brands have the tightest cross-channel messaging?"

**Pass criterion:** Industry professionals rate the ranking as "mostly correct" (at least 4 of 5 brands ranked in a way they find plausible). Perfect agreement isn't expected — the goal is face validity.

**Failure mode to watch for:** If professionals disagree with each other about the ranking, the concept of "messaging coherence" may not be well-defined enough to build a product around. This would be the most important negative finding — it would mean the metric measures something real but not something people recognize or care about.

**Estimated effort:** 3-5 days (document collection + scheduling expert feedback sessions)

## Decision Framework

After the five experiments, we'll be in one of four positions:

**All five pass:** The methodology works on real documents, provides channel-level and attribute-level diagnostics, captures temporal dynamics, and produces competitive rankings that professionals find credible. Proceed to packaging as a commercial research offering.

**Experiments 1-2 pass, 3+ fail:** The methodology works as a high-level coherence score and can identify outlier channels, but can't drill into attribute-level detail or temporal dynamics. This is still a viable product — a simpler "coherence scorecard" rather than a deep diagnostic. Scope the offering accordingly.

**Experiment 1 fails:** The fundamental measurement doesn't work on real documents. Either the technique needs significant modification (e.g., different layers, different pooling, anisotropy correction) or the idea doesn't transfer from controlled stimuli to real-world text. Debug before proceeding — revisit the representation extraction approach.

**Experiments pass but 5 fails (experts don't recognize the ranking):** The metric measures something real but not something the market cares about. Pivot the framing — instead of "messaging coherence," look for what the metric IS capturing that professionals find surprising or useful. The most interesting products often emerge from "we measured X and found Y instead."

## Immediate Next Steps

1. **Select the 10 products for Experiment 1.** Need 5 "known consistent" and 5 "known inconsistent" CPG brands. Use professional judgment and public information. Bias toward products where we have confidence in the ground truth.

2. **Collect real documents.** For each product, gather 3-5 documents from different channels: product website, regulatory/ingredient panel, Amazon listing, a social media post (brand's own), and a consumer review. Store as structured data (product ID, channel, text, URL, date collected).

3. **Adapt the extraction pipeline.** The current pipeline expects our stimuli JSON format. Need a lightweight adapter that takes real documents (variable length, no controlled attributes) and runs them through the same extraction and RSA pipeline. This is a few hours of work on top of the existing codebase.

4. **Run Experiment 1.** If it passes, proceed to 2. If it fails, debug.

## What This Plan Deliberately Does Not Include

- Platform architecture, pricing models, or go-to-market strategy. Those are premature until the methodology is validated.
- Patent filings. The technique builds on published academic work (RSA, representation engineering, linear probes). File only if the specific application to brand coherence proves novel and defensible after validation.
- Hiring. This is a one-person research effort using AI tooling (as demonstrated by the experiment that produced these findings).
- Fundraising. The validation experiments cost nothing beyond time. The first commercial engagement funds itself.
